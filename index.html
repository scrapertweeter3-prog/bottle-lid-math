The bottle outlives the lid

Insulated bottles are bought on ice-retention hours, and the wall earns them: vacuum between two layers of steel moves almost no heat. The failure lives in the lid, the one part with moving pieces and gaskets. A silicone seal compresses year after year until the bottle leaks, or a flip-top hinge cracks, or a spout valve stiffens. None of it touches the vacuum, which is why a dead lid reads as a dead bottle when it is a four-dollar part.

The replacement ledger is small and decisive. A $35 bottle whose gasket gives out at year two and gets retired whole runs five bottles a decade, $175. The same bottle kept alive on replacement seals needs one body and five $4 gaskets, $55 across the same ten years: $5.50 a year against $17.50. The 3.2x spread is the price of throwing away a working vacuum because rubber compressed.

Warranty fine print draws the same line. Bottle warranties cover the steel and manufacturing defects; wear items like gaskets and hinges sit on the exclusion list by name, and the brands worth buying sell the parts anyway. A spare-parts page stocked with $4 seals is the tell that the maker expects the body to outlive several of them, and it is the cheapest repairability signal to check before paying.

Failure-mode shopping prices the wear item before the sticker: what dies first, what the replacement costs, whether the maker sells it. The reviews ranked by how things fail at DurablePicks run that ledger across the rest of the household kit.
